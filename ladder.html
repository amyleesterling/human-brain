From a cortex to a synapse

Five orders of magnitude of one human brain, in a single move. Every rung is a real measurement already on this site, and the number in the corner is the true width of what you are looking at, not a caption.

The six rungs

A whole brain, 15 cm. The Allen human reference atlas as packaged by the HuBMAP Human Reference Atlas: 283 named structures, the outer surface drawn as glass so the thalamus, hippocampus, brainstem and cerebellum inside it are visible.

The cortex, 17 cm. The Human Connectome Project's group-average surface, built from MRI across more than a thousand people. Just the folded sheet now, about 2 mm thick, and spread flat it would cover a large dinner napkin. Two thirds of it is buried inside the folds.

The cable underneath, 18 cm. 87 named white matter bundles from the same project, averaged over 1,065 subjects. Everything the cortex says to itself goes through here.

One cubic millimetre, 3.9 mm. H01: a piece of a living woman's temporal cortex, with all 49,379 of its cell bodies in the place the microscope found them. Photographing this much tissue took 1.4 petabytes.

One neuron, 2.1 mm. A layer 5 pyramidal cell from that block, followed by hand through the whole volume, carrying 3,347 incoming synapses.

One synapse, 3 µm. An axon ending and the dendrite it is talking to, pulled straight out of the electron microscopy. The gap between them is about 20 nanometres.

The first three rungs are all the same size, and that is not a mistake. A brain measures 15 cm, its cortex 17 and its white matter 18, because they are one organ looked at three ways rather than three scales. The readout says 15, 17 and 18 centimetres in turn rather than inventing a descent that is not there. The ladder only starts descending at the fourth rung, and then it falls off a cliff.

Why this is six scenes and not one

A cortex is 0.2 metres. A synaptic cleft is 0.00000002 metres. That is seven orders of magnitude, and a 32-bit float carries about seven significant digits, so a single scene holding both ends would lose the synapse to rounding error before it ever reached the screen.

So each rung is drawn normalised to its own size, and its true extent in metres travels alongside it. The zoom you see is continuous; the arithmetic behind it never spans more than one rung at a time. The readout is computed from the real measurements, so it cannot drift away from the picture.

The jump from a cell to a synapse is the big one. Everything else on this page steps down by a factor of a few. That one steps down by seven hundred, and there is no dishonest way to smooth it: a neuron really is that much larger than the junctions covering it. The cell you just left has 3,347 of them arriving on it, and this is one.

Nothing here is glossy. These cells were submerged in fluid, and submerged tissue has almost no refractive step at its surface, so it should read as soft and translucent rather than as painted plastic with a highlight. The rendering uses a real three-point light rig with high roughness and no metalness, and keeps emission low, because pushing emission up to make things visible is what flattens a picture and kills every shadow.
The colours are ordered, not decorative. The cell bodies in the cubic millimetre are tinted by their depth below the surface, on a ramp of explicit stops that climbs in lightness from deep blue through violet to gold. Walking a blue-to-gold ramp round a hue wheel instead would pass through green, and green would appear in the picture carrying a meaning neither end of the scale contains.
